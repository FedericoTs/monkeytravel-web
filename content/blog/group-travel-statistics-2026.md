# Group Travel Statistics 2026: 69% of Trips Are Planned Together

Travel media loves the solo travel narrative — and solo travel is real. But when we looked at **1,651 actual trip-planning sessions from summer 2026**, the story the data tells is the opposite of the one the headlines tell.

Travel, by the numbers, is a group activity.

## The Headline Numbers

Among travelers who told us who they were traveling with:

- **69% were planning a trip with other people** — a partner, friends, or family
- **31% were planning solo**

Seven in ten trips have more than one stakeholder. That's not a niche; that's the market. And it reframes a lot of what we think we know about why trips do or don't happen.

## Why This Number Matters More Than It Looks

A solo trip has one decision-maker. A group trip has a committee — and committees have failure modes that no destination guide addresses:

- **Dates multiply.** One person's free week has to intersect four other calendars.
- **Budgets diverge.** The friend who wants the nice hotel and the friend who wants the hostel are on the same trip.
- **Nobody wants to be the dictator.** So decisions default to the group chat, where they go to die — forty messages about Lisbon vs Barcelona, zero bookings.
- **One person becomes the unpaid travel agent.** Usually the one reading this. The planner does the research, builds the plan, fields the objections, and burns out.

If any of that sounds familiar, it's because it's the *majority experience* of travel planning, not the exception. Reddit's travel communities are full of it — friends who "refuse to commit," trips that collapse while flight prices climb, group chats that produce chaos instead of itineraries.

## What the Group-Trip Data Suggests Actually Works

The group trips that make it out of the chat share three habits:

**1. A plan that exists somewhere.** Not "in the chat" — an actual itinerary anyone can open. The single biggest killer of group trips is that the plan has no home, so every discussion starts from zero. Put the plan in one place and share the link; that alone changes the dynamic. (This is exactly what a [shared group itinerary](/group-trip-planner) is for.)

**2. Voting instead of debating.** Groups don't need consensus on every dinner — they need a fast way to register preferences. Thumbs up or down on each activity beats forty messages, and the quiet members of the group actually get a say.

**3. One anchor decision made early.** Dates first, always. Every group trip that survives makes the dates non-negotiable early; every trip that dies keeps them "flexible" until flights double in price.

We wrote up the full playbook in [how to plan a group trip](/blog/how-to-plan-a-group-trip) — and if your crowd is more herd-of-cats than committee, [the mistakes to avoid](/blog/group-travel-mistakes-to-avoid) is the companion piece.

## Where Groups Go

Group-leaning destinations in our data skew toward places that offer *parallel* experiences — where the beach person, the museum person, and the nightlife person can all have a good day and reunite for dinner. [Barcelona](/destinations/barcelona) is the archetype. [Bangkok](/destinations/bangkok) and [Lisbon](/destinations/lisbon) fit the same pattern: dense, varied, forgiving of split itineraries.

Solo-leaning plans, by contrast, over-index on immersion destinations — [Tokyo](/destinations/tokyo) and [Kyoto](/destinations/kyoto) draw a disproportionate share of one-person itineraries, which fits: they reward the traveler who can wander without negotiating.

## The Takeaway

The travel industry builds for parties of one and sells to parties of five. The 69% deserves better tooling than a group chat and a shared spreadsheet.

If you're the planner — and if you've read this far, you are — the fix isn't working harder. It's making the plan shared, votable, and visible from day one. [Start a trip](/trips/new), send the link, and let the group do what groups are actually good at: reacting.

*Data: 1,651 anonymized planning sessions on MonkeyTravel, May 31 – August 4, 2026. Group/solo percentages computed over sessions that answered; aggregate data only, no individual travelers identifiable. Full findings in the [Q3 2026 Travel Planning Report](/blog/q3-2026-travel-planning-report).*
