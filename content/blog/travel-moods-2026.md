# The Moods of Travel in 2026: What People Actually Want From a Trip

When you plan a trip on MonkeyTravel, one of the first things you do is pick the trip's mood — the interests that should shape the itinerary. Culture or nightlife, food or nature, adventure or relaxation. Most people pick around five.

Those choices are a quiet kind of honesty. Nobody performs for a planning tool the way they perform on Instagram — you pick what you actually want your days to contain. So we aggregated the mood selections from **261 real trips planned in summer 2026**, and the result is a portrait of what travel is *for* right now.

It's not the portrait the marketing suggests.

## The Ranking

Share of trips that selected each mood:

1. **Culture — 74%**
2. **Art — 68%**
3. **Photography — 66%**
4. **Food — 57%**
5. **Adventure — 56%**
6. **Nature — 56%**
7. **History — 56%**
8. **Nightlife — 33%**
9. **Shopping — 32%**
10. **Relaxation — 22%**

The average trip carries about five moods — nobody travels for just one thing.

## The Relaxation Paradox

Look at the bottom of that list. **Relaxation — the word on every resort brochure and half of all out-of-office replies — was picked by barely one trip in five.**

We don't think travelers are lying on the beach less. We think "relaxing" is how people describe a trip *afterward*, not what they build it around. When someone sits down with real dates and a real destination, what they actually plan for is stimulation: things to see, eat, photograph, and learn. Rest is the byproduct of a good trip, not its architecture.

There's a practical lesson in that. If you plan a trip *around* relaxation — no anchors, no reservations, "we'll see" — you usually get boredom with better weather. The trips that end up feeling restful are the ones with a light but real structure: one thing worth doing each day and space around it.

## Culture Is the Default Setting

Three of the top four moods — culture, art, photography — describe the same traveler: someone who wants to *witness* a place, not consume it. That squares with what we see in [where people are going](/blog/q3-2026-travel-planning-report): Tokyo and Kyoto keep winning precisely because they reward attention. The camera stat is worth pausing on — two-thirds of trips now list photography as a planning priority, which means the itinerary question isn't just "what do we do?" but "what will we want to remember seeing?"

Food, at 57%, is the strongest single-interest signal in the data — and it's even stronger when you look at [what actually ends up inside the itineraries](/blog/how-many-activities-per-day-itinerary), where food stops are a third of everything planned. Nightlife, meanwhile, is a minority interest at 33% — the 2026 traveler is far more likely to plan around a market than a club.

## What This Means for Your Next Trip

**Pick your real moods, not your aspirational ones.** The itinerary you'll actually enjoy comes from the interests you actually have. If you never visit museums at home, three museums in Rome won't fix that.

**Combine, don't choose.** The data says the normal trip is culture + food + something outdoors. Destinations that do all three well — Rome, Bangkok, Barcelona, Tokyo — keep topping the [planning charts](/blog/most-planned-destinations-2026) for exactly that reason.

**Don't schedule relaxation. Protect it.** Leave one morning in three unplanned. That's the version of rest that survives contact with a real trip.
