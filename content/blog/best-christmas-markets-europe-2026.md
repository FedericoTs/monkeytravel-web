# Europe's Best Christmas Markets 2026: The Dates That Are Actually Confirmed

There is a problem with Christmas market articles, and it is worth naming before you book anything. Search for "Strasbourg Christmas market 2026" today and you will find several confident-looking sites telling you it runs 27 November to 27 December. We checked the city's own site on 3 September 2026. It still displays the **2025** dates. Nobody has announced 2026 yet — the numbers being published are extrapolations dressed up as facts, and if you book flights around them you are betting on somebody's guess.

So this guide is built the other way round. Every date below was read off the organiser's own page on 3 September 2026, and the markets that have not announced are listed as exactly that, with what their official site currently shows. Five of Europe's best are confirmed. Four are not. Both lists are useful, and the second one is the one nobody else will give you.

## Confirmed 2026 dates

| Market | 2026 dates | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Vienna** — Rathausplatz | **13 Nov – 26 Dec** | Daily 10:00–22:00; 24 Dec until 18:30 |
| **Cologne** — Markt am Dom | **16 Nov – 23 Dec** *(provisional)* | Closed 22 Nov (Totensonntag) |
| **Colmar** — Alsace | **23 Nov – 29 Dec** | Runs four days past Christmas |
| **Nuremberg** — Christkindlesmarkt | **27 Nov – 24 Dec** | Opening ceremony 27 Nov, 17:30 |
| **Zagreb** — Advent u Zagrebu | **28 Nov – 7 Jan 2027** | The longest season of the five |

One caveat, and it is the kind this guide exists for: Cologne labels its 2026 dates *Vorr.* — short for *voraussichtlich*, provisional. They are the organiser's own numbers and unlikely to move far, but they are not final, and no other site quoting them says so.

Two things jump out of that table. The season is far wider than "December" — Vienna opens on 13 November and Zagreb does not close until 7 January, which is nearly two months of market. And the openings cluster: three of the five fall inside one week, 23–28 November.

That clustering is not coincidence, and understanding why lets you predict the markets that have not announced.

## How to work out an unannounced date

European Christmas markets are anchored to Advent, not to a fixed calendar date. **The first Sunday of Advent in 2026 is 29 November.** Work back from that and most German-speaking markets open in the week before it — typically the Monday or the Friday.

The Sunday before First Advent is **Totensonntag, 22 November 2026**, a day of remembrance in Germany. Markets that have already opened close for it. Cologne's official site confirms exactly that: open from 16 November, shut on 22 November, reopening after. That closure is a useful cross-check — it independently confirms the Advent dating, and it is the kind of detail that ruins a day trip if you do not know it.

So for a German market with no announced date, "opens the week of 23–27 November, closes 23 or 24 December" is a well-founded estimate. Treat it as an estimate, not a booking.

## The four that have not announced yet

As of 3 September 2026, these had not published 2026 dates. Do not trust a third-party site that claims otherwise.

- **Strasbourg** — the official site still shows *26 November – 24 December 2025*. France's oldest Christmas market, running since 1570, and the one most often quoted with invented 2026 dates.
- **Budapest, Advent Feast at the Basilica** — the official site still shows *14 November 2025 – 1 January 2026*.
- **Dresden, Striezelmarkt** — the city's page was unreachable when we checked. Germany's oldest market, first held in 1434.
- **Basel** — the market's domain now redirects to the city tourism site, which blocked our check.

For all four, apply the Advent rule above and re-check the official page in October, which is when most announcements land.

## The five confirmed markets, and whether they are worth the trip

### 1. Vienna, Rathausplatz — 13 November to 26 December

The one that opens first and feels least like a market and most like a city-scale production: the neo-Gothic town hall lit from below, an ice rink threading through the park behind it, and a tree-lined avenue of stalls. Vienna's real advantage is density — the Rathausplatz market is the headline, but Spittelberg, Karlsplatz and the Freyung are all a short tram ride apart and all quite different in character.

**Cost reality:** a mug of *Punsch* runs about €5–6 plus a deposit of €3–5 on the cup, refunded when you return it. Mid-range hotels in the first district climb steeply through December; the week of 13–20 November is meaningfully cheaper and the market is already fully open.

**The trade-off:** it is the most crowded of the five on December weekends, and the Rathausplatz stalls skew touristy. Go on a weekday, and treat Spittelberg as the antidote.

### 2. Cologne, Markt am Dom — 16 November to 23 December

The setting does the work here. The cathedral is one of the largest Gothic structures in Europe and the market sits directly at its foot, so the stalls are dwarfed by black stone rising into the dark. Cologne runs several markets within walking distance — the harbour one, the Alter Markt, the Neumarkt "Angels' Market" — and a single afternoon can take in three or four.

**Cost reality:** *Glühwein* around €4.50–5.50 plus a €3–5 cup deposit. Cologne is a comparatively cheap German city to sleep in outside trade-fair weeks, so the whole trip runs below Vienna or Munich.

**The trade-off:** remember the 22 November closure. And the cathedral market is right on top of the main station, which makes it convenient and busy in equal measure — arrive before 16:00 if you want to move freely.

### 3. Colmar, Alsace — 23 November to 29 December

Colmar is the one people come back from evangelising about. The old town is genuinely medieval, small enough to cross on foot, and the markets are spread across several squares rather than concentrated into one, so the whole centre becomes the venue rather than one crowded plaza. It also runs latest of the German-French group — four days past Christmas, when almost everything else has shut.

**Cost reality:** Alsace is not cheap, and Colmar hotels sell out early for December weekends. Staying in Strasbourg or across the Rhine and taking the train in is a common workaround.

**The trade-off:** small town, big reputation. December Saturdays are genuinely uncomfortable. Weekdays, or the run between Christmas and 29 December, are a completely different experience.

### 4. Nuremberg, Christkindlesmarkt — 27 November to 24 December

The most famous of them all, and the most traditional in a specific and enforced way: the market has rules about what may be sold, which keeps out the plastic tat that has crept into some of its rivals. It opens with a ceremony at 17:30 on 27 November, when the Nuremberg Christkind reads a prologue from the balcony of the Frauenkirche. Regular hours are 10:00 to 21:00.

**Cost reality:** Nuremberg's *Drei im Weggla* — three small bratwurst in a roll — is the cheapest satisfying meal at any market on this list. Accommodation is the pressure point; the city is not large and December fills it.

**The trade-off:** it closes on 24 December, earlier than Vienna, Colmar or Zagreb, and it is the single most crowded market in Germany. Its fame is deserved and it is also the reason to consider going somewhere else.

### 5. Zagreb, Advent u Zagrebu — 28 November to 7 January 2027

The outlier, and the value pick. Zagreb's Advent spread out of the main square into parks, courtyards and the tunnel under the upper town, and it is as much a series of outdoor bars and concerts as a shopping market. It also runs six weeks — a fortnight longer than anything else here — and stays open right through New Year to 7 January.

**Cost reality:** the cheapest of the five by a wide margin. Food, drink and accommodation all run well below Vienna or Colmar, and Croatia uses the euro, so there is no currency friction for eurozone visitors.

**The trade-off:** it is not a picture-book medieval market and does not try to be — the appeal is atmosphere and price rather than half-timbered houses. If you want the postcard, go to Colmar.

## Practical tips

- **The cup deposit is not a scam.** Nearly every German and Austrian market charges €3–5 on the mug and refunds it. Keep the cup if you want the souvenir; otherwise return it, and do not be surprised by the first bill.
- **Weekday afternoons, not December Saturdays.** Every market on this list is transformed by timing. The difference between a Tuesday at 16:00 and a Saturday at 19:00 is not marginal — it is the difference between wandering and shuffling.
- **The mid-November window is underrated.** Vienna and Cologne are fully open from 13 and 16 November, before school holidays and before flight prices climb. You get the same market at a materially lower price.
- **Pair markets by rail, not by air.** Cologne–Nuremberg is straightforward by train, as is Colmar–Strasbourg. Two markets in one trip almost always beats one market and two flights.
- **Re-check unannounced dates in October.** Strasbourg, Budapest, Dresden and Basel had all published by mid-autumn in previous years.
- **Plan around the rest of the month too.** Markets are an evening activity — see what else is worth doing in [December](/blog/where-to-go-in-december) and how the season sits in the [2026 travel calendar](/blog/2026-travel-calendar).

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

### When do Christmas markets open in Europe in 2026?

Earlier than most people expect. Vienna's Rathausplatz market opens on 13 November 2026 and Cologne on 16 November. The main cluster falls in the last week of November: Colmar on 23 November, Nuremberg on 27 November and Zagreb on 28 November. That timing is set by Advent — the first Sunday of Advent in 2026 is 29 November, and most markets open in the week before it.

### Which European Christmas markets have confirmed their 2026 dates?

As of 3 September 2026, five of the best-known had published dates on their own sites: Vienna (13 Nov – 26 Dec), Cologne (16 Nov – 23 Dec), Colmar (23 Nov – 29 Dec), Nuremberg (27 Nov – 24 Dec) and Zagreb (28 Nov – 7 Jan 2027) — Cologne's are marked provisional by the organiser. Strasbourg, Budapest, Dresden and Basel had not, and their official pages were still showing 2025 dates or were unavailable.

### Which Christmas markets are still open after Christmas Day?

Three of the five. Vienna runs to 26 December, Colmar to 29 December, and Zagreb all the way to 7 January 2027. Nuremberg closes on 24 December and Cologne on 23 December. If you are travelling between Christmas and New Year, Zagreb is the safest bet on this list.

### What is the cheapest European Christmas market to visit?

Zagreb, comfortably. Food, drink and accommodation all run well below the German, Austrian and Alsatian markets, and it uses the euro. Among the German-speaking markets, Cologne is generally the cheapest place to sleep outside trade-fair weeks.

### Why do Christmas markets close for a day in late November?

That is Totensonntag, a German day of remembrance falling on the Sunday before Advent — 22 November in 2026. Markets that have already opened, such as Cologne, close for it and reopen the following day. It catches out visitors who arrive on that specific Sunday.
