Ten days, three cities. Rome, Florence, Venice. It sounds like three trips of a bit over three days each.

It is not. It is about seven usable days and three spent moving, and which of those you get depends almost entirely on decisions you make before you book anything.

Multi-city trips do not fail on the cities. Everyone picks good cities. They fail on the joins.

## The transit tax nobody budgets for

Here is the arithmetic people skip.

A "travel day" between two European cities is rarely two hours. It is checkout, the trip to the station or airport, the buffer you leave so you are not sprinting, the journey itself, the trip into the new city, and check-in. Then it is finding food somewhere unfamiliar while carrying a bag.

Realistically that consumes most of a day, and it consumes the two most useful parts of it: the morning you would have spent at a museum and the evening you would have spent at dinner.

Two joins in a ten-day trip is not "a bit of travelling." It is 20 to 30 percent of your holiday.

**The rule that follows:** every additional city costs you roughly one full day. If a city is not worth a full day of pure transit on top of the time you spend there, it does not belong on the route.

## How many days per city

There is no universal number, but there is a useful floor: **two nights minimum, three for anywhere you actually care about.**

One night in a city means you arrive in the afternoon, sleep, and leave in the morning. You have not visited it. You have slept in it and paid for the privilege of a travel day on each side.

A rough working guide:

- **2 nights** — a small city, or a stop that exists to break a long journey
- **3 nights** — enough for the main sights plus one neighbourhood at a normal pace
- **4-5 nights** — a major capital, or anywhere you want day trips from
- **A week or more** — somewhere you are using as a base rather than touring

Then subtract. If your ten days give you three cities at three nights each, you have used nine nights and two travel days, and you have overbooked. Two cities at four and five nights is a better trip almost every time.

## Order the route, do not just pick it

Once the cities are chosen, the order is the next decision and it is worth more than people think.

**Go in a line, not a loop.** The classic mistake is flying into a capital, touring outward, and doubling back to the same airport because that is where the return flight is. That backtrack is a whole extra travel day spent revisiting ground you have covered.

**Which brings us to open-jaw flights.** You fly into one city and out of another. Almost every airline and booking site supports it, most people never look for it, and on a three-city trip it routinely saves an entire day. If you are doing Rome, Florence, Venice, fly into Rome and out of Venice. Do not fly home from Rome.

**Put the long journey where it hurts least.** If one leg is much longer than the others, place it where a lost day costs you least — often immediately after arrival while you are still adjusting, rather than in the middle when you are in rhythm.

**End somewhere easy.** Your last city should have a straightforward airport connection. Finishing in a place with a two-hour transfer to the airport turns your final morning into logistics.

## Getting between cities

**Train, where it exists.** In most of Europe and Japan, city-centre to city-centre rail beats flying for anything under roughly four hours, once you count airport transfers and security. The journey is also usable time: you can eat, read, and look out of the window.

**Budget flights, with the real cost attached.** A 40-euro flight is not a 40-euro journey once you add two airport transfers, bag fees and the ninety minutes you spend early. Compare door to door, not gate to gate.

**Buses and ferries for the awkward joins.** Sometimes the only sensible link between two places is a bus, and there is no shame in it. Just put it in the plan as a half-day rather than pretending it is a transfer.

Book the intercity legs before you build the daily itinerary. The times you actually get will reshape your first and last day in every city, and building the days first means rebuilding them afterwards.

## Build days around arrival and departure, not around a wishlist

The single most common failure in multi-city itineraries is treating arrival day and departure day as full days.

They are not. Arrival day is an afternoon and an evening, and you will be tired and carrying a bag. Departure day is a morning, and part of it belongs to packing.

Plan accordingly:

1. **Arrival day** — one easy thing near the accommodation. A walk, a market, a neighbourhood dinner. Nothing ticketed, nothing across town.
2. **Full days** — three or four stops, grouped geographically, with realistic gaps.
3. **Departure day** — one thing at most, and only if it is near where you are staying or on the way out.

A three-night stop therefore has one real day, one strong day, and two half days. That is the honest shape, and planning against it beats discovering it.

## The mistakes that cost the most

**Booking accommodation before fixing the route.** Non-refundable hotel nights are the thing that locks a bad sequence in place. Route first.

**One-night stops.** Covered above, and it is the most common regret.

**Ignoring luggage.** Every join is a day of carrying everything you brought. Multi-city is the trip where packing light stops being advice and becomes structural.

**Treating each city as a separate plan.** Three individual itineraries stapled together will double-book your energy — three "big museum days" in a row, no recovery, all the walking-heavy days consecutive. Plan the trip as one arc.

**Forgetting the checked-bag maths on budget carriers.** Two intra-Europe flights with bags can quietly cost more than the rail alternative that would also have saved you two airport transfers.

## A working checklist

1. Cut the city list until every remaining stop is worth a full transit day
2. Assign nights: two minimum, three for anywhere that matters
3. Order the cities in a line, not a loop
4. Search flights as open-jaw — in to the first city, out of the last
5. Book the intercity legs and note the real arrival and departure times
6. Only now build the daily plans, with arrival and departure days treated as halves
7. Book accommodation last, once the route is fixed

## Frequently asked questions

**How many cities should I visit in two weeks?**
Three is comfortable, four is busy, five is a blur. Each city costs roughly a full day in transit on top of your time there, so five cities in fourteen days means about five days spent moving. Two or three stops with real time in each almost always makes a better trip.

**What is an open-jaw flight?**
A ticket that flies you into one city and home from a different one. It stops you backtracking to your arrival airport at the end of a trip, which on a multi-city route usually saves a full day. Most booking sites support it under a "multi-city" or "different return airport" option.

**Is it cheaper to book multi-city flights together or separately?**
It varies, and it is worth checking both. A single multi-city ticket often prices better than separate one-ways on the same carrier and protects you if a leg is delayed. Separate budget-airline tickets can be cheaper but leave you unprotected if one leg fails.

**How many days do I need in each city?**
Two nights is the practical floor and three is better for anywhere you actually want to see. One night means arriving in the afternoon and leaving in the morning, which is not a visit.

**Should I take trains or flights between cities?**
In most of Europe and Japan, take the train for journeys under about four hours. City-centre to city-centre rail usually beats flying once you count airport transfers and security, and the time on board is usable.

**What order should I visit the cities in?**
Go in a line rather than a loop, put the longest journey where losing a day hurts least, and finish in a city with an easy airport connection so your last morning is not spent on logistics.
